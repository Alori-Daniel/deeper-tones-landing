Wash gently, pat dry and moisturise while skin is still damp, using a thick cream or ointment [3][4][5]. Keep sunscreen on, and look after lips, hands and heels. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) says you should see some improvement within two weeks [3]. Keep the routine up while the air stays dry, and for eczema carry on even when skin improves [10].

Harmattan is the dry, dusty season in West Africa, and a paper on it links the season with dried skin and chapped lips [1]. A cold, dry winter in the UK or US can do something similar: a review found that people in northern Europe and North America may get dry, itchy skin in winter [2].

## Why does my skin go dry and ashy in harmattan?

Low humidity and low temperatures lead to a general decrease in skin barrier function, a review of the research found [2]. The barrier is the skin's outer layer. When it works less well, skin is more vulnerable to rubbing and irritation [2]. The AAD says dry skin is damaged skin, which is why gentle care matters [4].

On darker skin, dryness is easy to see. Flaking, discolouration and rough texture are more noticeable, and the skin can look whitish or grayish-white [3]. That ashy look is dry skin showing, not something you did wrong. A moisturiser with ceramides, ingredients that help seal in moisture, can reduce it [3].

## What is a simple routine for dry skin?

Think of three steps: wash gently, pat dry and leave skin a little damp, then seal it in.

{{figure:lock-water-in}}

**Wash gently.** Use a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser only where you need it, such as the armpits and groin, and not so much that you see a thick lather [4]. Deodorant soaps and products with alcohol or fragrance can irritate dry skin [3][4]. Keep showers and baths short, about 5 to 10 minutes, in warm water, not hot [3][4].

**Pat dry, leave it damp.** Rubbing can irritate very dry skin, so pat dry with a clean towel [4]. Leave the skin slightly damp, then moisturise while skin is still damp to lock that water in [3]. The NHS gives the same advice: apply the moisturiser straight away, while the skin is still moist [5].

**Seal it in with cream or ointment.** Thicker creams and ointments work better than thin lotions [3][4]. The NHS says ointments hold moisture best but feel greasy, lotions are the least greasy but can be less effective, and creams sit in between [5]. For very dry skin, one dermatologist quoted by the AAD recommends petroleum jelly (petrolatum) or a thick cream, and another says petroleum jelly traps existing moisture in the skin [3].

The AAD lists shea butter as an ingredient in a cream or ointment [4]. The first dermatologist says butters and oils tend to liquefy on the skin, so they are less effective as moisturisers [3]. Reapply through the day if your skin is extremely dry [3].

> **Safety:** Emollients (moisturisers) can catch fire more easily on clothing or bedding, so do not smoke or go near naked flames [10]. They make bath water very slippery, and if your skin reacts, stop and speak to a pharmacist [5]. If pregnant or breastfeeding, check any new active ingredient with a pharmacist or doctor first.

## What about lips, hands and heels?

**Lips.** Cold weather, dry air or wind can leave lips dry or sore, and licking them can make them sorer [6]. The NHS suggests a lip balm containing petroleum jelly or beeswax, and one with sun protection when you are outdoors [6].

**Hands.** Hands are often the first place dry skin shows [4]. Use a fragrance-free hand cream after every wash [3], and wear gloves for dishes or wet jobs, and in cold, dry weather [4].

**Heels.** Dry skin can crack and even bleed [4], and cracked heels are common, especially in winter [7]. Moisturise damp heels soon after washing. At bedtime, apply plain petroleum jelly and wear socks to keep it off your sheets [7]. Avoid open-heeled and worn-down shoes [7].

## Do I still need sunscreen in harmattan?

Keep it in your routine. The AAD includes a broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher in its dry-skin advice [4]. The WHO says UV is higher the closer you are to the equator, and can be high even with cloud cover [8].

Harmattan dust can make the air hazy, with low visibility [1]. We found no research showing that makes sunscreen unnecessary, so we would keep wearing it. Our guide to [sunscreen for dark skin](/guides/sunscreen-for-dark-skin) explains what to look for on a label.

## What about dust and my eyes?

Dust is part of harmattan, which brings significant dust from the Sahara [1]. If your eyes turn red, itchy, gritty or watery, it may be conjunctivitis, which is caused by infection or allergies [9]. If it is infectious, the NHS says to wash your hands regularly and not to rub your eyes, to stop it spreading [9].

## How do I handle eczema in the dry season?

Cold, dry weather increases the prevalence and risk of flares of eczema, also called atopic dermatitis [2]. On darker skin, eczema can look dark brown, purple or grayish [11]. In older children and teens, bumps on darker skin tend to look gray to violet-brown [11]. It is intensely itchy [11].

The NHS suggests moisturising as often as possible and carrying on even when the eczema improves [10]. Wash with a moisturiser instead of soap, and keep cool, because being hot can make eczema itchier [10]. Heat and changes in temperature are listed triggers [10].

## What should I avoid?

**Hot water and rubbing.** Hot water and long soaks make dryness worse [3]. Rubbing your skin dry can irritate it [4].

**Fragrance, alcohol and retinoids.** The AAD says to stop using skin care products that contain alcohol (except hand sanitiser), fragrance (including deodorant soaps) or retinoids when your skin is dry [4]. Retinoids are vitamin A-based skin treatments.

**Bleaching and "toning" creams.** A review of lab studies found damaged or diseased skin may absorb a modest amount more than intact skin [12]. The FDA advises not using over-the-counter skin lightening products. It says there are no FDA-approved or legally marketed ones, that repeated use can allow mercury to be absorbed into the bloodstream, and that it has had reports of rashes, facial swelling and discolouration, which may be permanent, with hydroquinone products [13].

> **Safety:** Dry, cracked skin is a reason to moisturise, not to use a strong cream. If you already use a bleaching or "toning" cream, read how to [stop a bleaching cream safely](/guides/stop-bleaching-cream-safely) and ask a professional for help. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask a pharmacist or doctor before changing what you use.

## When to see a professional

Get help the same day (in the UK, an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111) if:

- you have eczema that is blistered, crusty, leaking fluid or has spots filled with pus [10]
- your eczema is painful, swollen or feels warm [10]
- your eczema suddenly gets worse or bigger, or you have a high temperature or feel generally unwell [10]
- eczema brings dome-shaped blisters and bumps. The AAD says this can be a serious infection that needs immediate medical care in children, babies and anyone with a weakened immune system [11]
- you have eye pain, sensitivity to light or changes in vision [9]
- a crack or patch of dry skin becomes painful, hot and swollen, which can be an infection called cellulitis. Redness can be harder to see on brown or black skin [14]

Otherwise, see a doctor, pharmacist or dermatologist if:

- cracks are severe, keep bleeding, or do not improve with the steps above [4][7]
- dryness carries on after home care. It can be eczema, ichthyosis or psoriasis, skin conditions that all cause very dry skin, which some people with darker skin live with for years without a diagnosis [3]
- eczema is not settling with treatment [10]
- you see yellow or golden crusts. The AAD says to contact a dermatologist's office [11]
- lips are hot, painful and swollen, which could be a sign of infection [6]
- any product, including a moisturiser, stings or burns. Stop using it and ask for advice [4][5]
