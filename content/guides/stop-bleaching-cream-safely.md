This guide explains why a flare can happen, why skin can look darker afterwards, and how to keep your routine plain while it settles.

> **Safety:** After long daily use of a strong lightening cream, a sudden stop can cause a flare, so it is best to stop with a doctor's or pharmacist's help [5].

## Why stopping can cause a flare

Labels on lightening creams are often wrong. When researchers tested creams bought in West African city markets, some held hydroquinone and a very strong steroid called clobetasol propionate, and the labels often did not match what was inside [9]. A steroid (corticosteroid) is a medicine that calms inflammation. The NHS says clobetasol is much stronger than other steroid treatments, and that it should only be used briefly on the face, because facial skin thins easily [5].

When skin has had a strong steroid for a long time and it is stopped, a rebound can follow. It can show up as redness, burning or stinging, itching, peeling, and sometimes oozing sores [5]. The UK medicines regulator (MHRA) says it is most common on delicate skin such as the face, and can start days to weeks after stopping [3][4]. A review of 34 studies found it reported mostly on the face, after long-term use of strong steroids, with burning and stinging the most common symptoms [6].

A study of people at a dermatology clinic in south-east Nigeria who used steroids to lighten their skin found the same pattern. Stopping usually brought a sore, inflamed skin reaction that patients found very unpleasant [7]. If that happened to you, it is a known pattern. It is not a failing on your part.

If your skin flared before and you went back to the cream, that may have been your skin reacting to stopping. It does not have to mean your skin needs the cream.

> **Safety:** Putting the cream back on calms the skin again, so going back can feel like the answer. The NHS says not to restart without talking to a doctor [5].

You cannot tell what is in a jar from its label [9]. It is safest to treat any lightening cream of unknown content as one that may contain a steroid.

## Why skin can look darker afterwards

If your skin looks darker in the weeks after stopping, there are a few possible reasons.

- **Redness you cannot easily see.** Redness is harder to see on brown and black skin [3][5]. Go by how your skin feels too: burning, stinging or soreness.
- **Marks left by inflammation.** Dark marks often follow irritated skin, and they are more common and more intense in darker skin. Marks in the top layer of skin can take months to years to fade without treatment [12].
- **Blue-grey patches from hydroquinone.** Hydroquinone is a lightening chemical. Long use has been linked to ochronosis, a blue-black or grey-blue, lace-like discolouration on the face, most often reported in people with darker skin [10]. The FDA says it may be permanent [2], and it is hard to treat [11]. If you see this pattern, a dermatologist should look at it.

## Why stopping is still worth it

Long use of a strong steroid on the face can thin the skin [5]. Steroid from creams can also be absorbed into the body. It can affect the adrenal glands, which make the body's own steroid hormones [5][8].

Some creams contain mercury, which can damage the kidneys and nerves [1][2]. Mercury cosmetics are banned in Nigeria and many other countries, but they are still sold [1]. In one study of women using mercury creams, urine mercury fell quickly after they stopped, and over three quarters of those who stopped went into remission from a kidney condition [1].

## Getting a plan, and keeping your routine plain

How to stop depends on what is in the cream and how long you used it. After long-term use, a doctor may lower the strength or how often it is used rather than stop it all at once [3][5]. A professional sets that plan. This guide does not give one.

What you can do alongside is keep your routine very plain:

- A gentle cleanser, with no scrub and no strong scent.
- A plain moisturiser.
- Sunscreen every morning, broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher. Sun can make dark marks deeper [12].

That is all. Anything that irritates can worsen dark marks [12].

### What to expect

Recovery is measured in months rather than days [3][4].

- **Days to weeks:** a flare may start. Burning more than itching, and redness that covers a whole area rather than coming in patches, are signs doctors look for [3].
- **Weeks to months:** the skin settles slowly. There is no fixed timeline, and the MHRA review describes symptoms lasting many months in some people [4].
- **Dark marks:** these fade more slowly than redness, over months to years [12].

## What not to do

- **Do not switch to another lightening product.** The FDA advises against over-the-counter skin lightening products, and none are approved for sale in the US [2]. Creams without mercury can still contain hydroquinone [1].
- **Do not scrub or peel.** Irritation can worsen dark marks [12], and steroid-thinned skin is delicate [5].
- **Do not pile on actives.** Actives are ingredients that change the skin, like acids, retinoids and strong vitamin C. On sore skin they can irritate and worsen dark marks [12].

## What a routine can and cannot do

A plain routine can calm your skin and, over months, help dark marks and uneven tone settle, as sunscreen helps keep them from deepening [12]. It cannot change the colour your skin naturally is, and no product should promise that.

## When to see a professional

Stopping is best done with a doctor or dermatologist if any of these apply:

- You used a strong steroid for a long time. Withdrawal reactions are mostly seen after at least daily use for more than a year [3].
- Your skin is very thin, sore, broken, or bruises easily. Thinning is a known effect of strong steroids [5].
- The cream was used near the eyes, or your eyes are affected.
- You may have been exposed to mercury. Signs can include tremors, tingling or numbness, headaches, unusual tiredness, or mood changes [2]. The ingredient list may say mercury, ammoniated mercury, mercurous chloride or mercuric iodide, but not always [1].
- You see blue-grey or blue-black patches [10].
- You are pregnant or breastfeeding. Mercury is a particular risk in pregnancy [2], and a pharmacist or doctor should guide any steroid decisions [5].
- You have pus-filled bumps, spreading redness, fever, or severe burning [3][5].
- You feel dizzy, faint or very tired [5].

If you can reach a dermatologist, good. If not, a general doctor or a pharmacist is a start. Take the jar, and tell them what you used, for how long, how often and where, and what happened each time you stopped.
