Melasma is a common skin condition that does not harm your body, with brown or greyish-brown patches, usually on the face [2][3]. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen and gentle care are the base of treatment, and results usually take 3 to 12 months [2][3]. It is more likely in medium to dark skin and in women [1][6]. It is long-term and often comes back, so the aim is patches that fade toward your own even tone [2][3].

## What is melasma?

Melasma is flat patches that can look tan, brown, grayish brown or bluish gray. They most often sit on the cheeks, forehead, chin and above the upper lip, and often appear on both sides of the face [1]. Many people first notice it during pregnancy or after starting birth control pills [1][3]. In pregnancy it is sometimes called the "mask of pregnancy" [1].

It is not contagious and not a sign of skin cancer [1]. It does not itch or hurt, though many people are upset by how it looks [3]. This is a pattern, not a diagnosis. A dermatologist can confirm it [1][3].

## What triggers melasma?

The exact cause is unknown. It involves pigment cells making too much melanin, the substance that gives skin its colour [3]. Several things appear to push them, a review found [4]. They include:

- **Sunlight.** Sun can darken existing patches and cause new ones [2].
- **Visible light.** One review says the blue and violet part of visible light, at high doses, can darken skin in darker skin types [4].
- **Hormones.** Pregnancy and hormonal medicines, such as contraceptive pills and hormone replacement, can contribute [3][4].
- **Heat.** Intense heat may help it persist, though solar heat has not been studied systematically [5].
- **Other triggers.** Stress [2] and some medicines [3] can trigger it.

## How is melasma different from other dark marks?

The marks that follow a pimple, a scratch or irritation are called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH. "Post-inflammatory" means after the skin was inflamed. A mark after a pimple usually sits where the spot was, in the same shape [8].

Melasma is not the mark a pimple or injury leaves behind, which is PIH [8]. It is typically flat patches on both sides of the face [1]. A review says pigment from sun or PIH generally fades once the trigger stops, while melasma usually does not [4]. The two can overlap, since people with melasma have PIH about 2.8 times more often [4].

## What helps melasma?

**Sun protection is the base.** The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) says sun protection can help fade melasma and prevent it from returning [2]. That means a wide-brimmed hat, shade, and a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher [2][3]. Broad-spectrum means it covers UVA and UVB rays. See our guide to [sunscreen for dark skin](/guides/sunscreen-for-dark-skin) for how to choose one.

**Choose a tint with iron oxide.** Iron oxide is a pigment that helps block visible light [9].

Reapply every two hours outdoors [3][9].

**Keep everything else gentle.** Irritation can make melasma worse [1], so choose gentle, fragrance-free products [3].

If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist before trying any of the ingredients below, and read the safety note further down. The AAD lists azelaic acid, vitamin C and kojic acid as gentler options [2]. The British Association of Dermatologists adds cysteamine and thiamidol, some sold without a prescription; all of these aim to reduce extra pigment [2][3].

A pooled analysis of 6 trials (673 people in total) found 20% azelaic acid did about as well as hydroquinone, a prescription medicine that stops pigment cells making melanin [3], with a slightly larger drop on one severity score [7]. More in our guide on [azelaic acid on dark skin](/guides/azelaic-acid-dark-skin).

If a non-prescription product has not helped after three to six months, the AAD says to see a dermatologist [1].

## What might a dermatologist suggest?

For stubborn melasma, a dermatologist may suggest prescription creams. These include hydroquinone, or a triple combination cream that mixes a retinoid (a vitamin A cream), a steroid and hydroquinone [2]. They may also suggest tranexamic acid tablets, a medicine usually used to stop bleeding [3], for hard-to-treat cases, after checking your health, since a past blood clot matters [2]. A review also flags a rare risk of deep vein clots, so people are screened first [6].

Peels, microneedling (tiny needle pricks) and lasers can sometimes be added [2]. The British Association of Dermatologists says peels can make pigmentation worse, lighten skin too much or scar, and should be done by an experienced practitioner, while lasers need a highly experienced operator [3].

> **Safety:** If you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding, ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist before using any treatment, including over-the-counter ones. Hydroquinone and retinoid creams should be avoided in pregnancy [3]. The AAD suggests waiting until after delivery to start treatment [1].

Sunscreen is the exception: a review calls tinted mineral sunscreens with iron oxide generally safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding [10]. A sunscreen maker funded that review and some of its authors work for the company, so ask your midwife or doctor if unsure.

## What can make melasma worse or harm your skin?

- **Unprotected sun.** It is common for melasma to return when you spend time outdoors without protecting your skin [1]. Sunbeds can worsen it too [3].
- **Irritation.** Some melasma treatments can irritate darker skin, which can make melasma worse and darker [1]. Stop any product that burns or stings [9].
- **Skin-lightening or bleaching creams.** Researchers found undisclosed steroids or mercury in some imported products, and the AAD says never to put liquid bleach on skin [9]. The British Association of Dermatologists says lightening creams must be used only when prescribed and supervised [3].
- **Steroid creams.** A review says a potent steroid cream cannot be used for long because of local side effects [5].
- **Unsupervised hydroquinone.** It can irritate, over-lighten or occasionally darken skin [3]. Long use of high concentrations has been linked to exogenous ochronosis, a form of skin darkening [5][6].

> **Safety:** Do not start hydroquinone, a steroid cream or any skin-lightening product for melasma without a prescriber's advice [3]. If your skin gets worse within 2 weeks of stopping a steroid cream, the UK medicines regulator (MHRA) says not to restart it without talking to your doctor [11].

## How long does it take, and will it come back?

The AAD says it usually takes 3 to 12 months to see results, and longer if you have had melasma for a long time [2]. Melasma can last for years or even a lifetime [2].

There is no cure at present, and it often returns after treatment stops [3]. A review calls it chronic and relapsing, so a maintenance routine, with daily sun protection first, matters [5]. If pregnancy or a medicine is the trigger, it may fade after delivery or after stopping, though it can return in a later pregnancy [1][3]. A review says it disappears in most women after menopause [5].

So the aim is patches that fade back toward your own skin tone, with fewer flare-ups, not lighter skin [2]. **Some treatments can lighten skin too much.** Hydroquinone, peels and lasers can over-lighten, which is one reason they belong with a dermatologist [3].

## When to see a professional

See a doctor, pharmacist or dermatologist if:

- you are not sure the patches are melasma, or they appeared with itching, soreness or a raised surface, since melasma is flat and has no symptoms [1][3]
- a non-prescription routine has not helped after three to six months [1]
- a mole or patch has changed [3]
- you have used skin-lightening or steroid creams, or your skin has become thin or fragile [3][9]
- a product stings or burns: stop it, and get advice if it keeps happening [9]
- you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding and want to use anything beyond sunscreen and gentle care [1][3]

In the UK, a GP can refer you to a dermatologist on the NHS if they are concerned [3]. Peels, microneedling and lasers are usually not on the NHS, so ask a doctor before booking [3].
