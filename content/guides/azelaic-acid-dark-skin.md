In the main 24-week trial on darker skin, the gain over a plain cream was clear by the end [1]. Below: what the studies show, a realistic before and after, strengths, side effects and how it compares with hydroquinone, an ingredient that reduces pigment production.

## What azelaic acid is

Azelaic acid is a simple acid that is a normal part of the human diet [8]. In skin care it slows tyrosinase, the enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction) your skin uses to make pigment, the substance that gives skin its colour [3][6]. It seems to act mainly on overactive pigment cells and has little effect on normal ones [7].

In the US, a 20% cream is approved for acne and a 15% gel for rosacea, a condition with facial redness and bumps [8][9]. Using it on dark marks or patches is an off-label use, meaning a use the regulator did not formally approve. It is still widely used for marks and patches [7].

## What studies on darker skin show

- **A 24-week trial, skin types IV to VI.** Skin types IV to VI are the darker half of the Fitzpatrick scale, a standard way to group skin by how it reacts to sun. After 24 weeks, 20% azelaic acid cream had reduced the strength of facial pigment more than the plain base cream (the vehicle). That held on a doctor's rating and a colour-measuring device [1].
- **A 16-week pilot study.** A 15% gel reduced both pimples and the marks after them. It had no comparison group, so treat it as a hint, not proof [3].
- **A review of treatments for skin of colour.** It found retinoids (vitamin A creams) and azelaic acid safe and beneficial for acne and the marks that follow [4].

Most of the strongest trials looked at [melasma](/guides/melasma-on-dark-skin), a patchy brown discolouration often on the cheeks and forehead, or at general facial pigment. Fewer studied marks after pimples, the pattern usually called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH, extra pigment after skin was inflamed). That is a limit on what we can promise.

## What a realistic before and after looks like

Expect fainter, not gone. Plan on 12 weeks for a first fair check and about six months for the real picture. A 2026 review puts typical overall improvement with 20% azelaic acid at roughly 25 to 50%, over about 12 to 16 weeks [6].

In practice, expect marks a little closer to your own tone, not a clean slate. The effect is mostly on overactive pigment cells, so the skin around the marks is not expected to change [7]. Rare colour loss is covered below. New marks keep arriving if the cause, such as picking or an irritating product, continues.

Before-and-after photos online are rarely taken in the same light or over the same weeks. Take your own, in the same spot by the same window, every four weeks.

If you were hoping for lighter skin overall, azelaic acid cannot do that. It can even out marks and patches toward your own colour, and that is a good result. No routine can change your baseline skin colour. ## 10% or 20%: strengths and countries

Reviews list azelaic acid products from 5% to 20%. The 15% and 20% ones are mostly sold as medicines, while some lower-strength products, such as 10% in one review of the Polish market, are sold as cosmetics [7]. The British Association of Dermatologists says some melasma creams, azelaic acid among the ingredients it lists, can be bought without a prescription [10]. Rules differ by country, so check the percentage on the label and ask a pharmacist what is sold where you live.

The trials on dark marks and patches used 15% or 20% [1][2][3]. We found no trial comparing 10% with 20% in darker skin. A 10% product may work less, or take longer, and we cannot say. Judge whichever you use with the same 12-week check.

For marks after pimples, our guides on [fading dark marks on dark skin](/guides/fade-dark-marks-dark-skin) and [how long dark spots take to fade](/guides/how-long-dark-spots-take-to-fade) cover timelines and what else helps, including [sunscreen](/guides/sunscreen-for-dark-skin).

## Side effects: stinging

Itching, burning and stinging are the common ones, most often at the start. In the 24-week trial, burning was slightly higher than with the plain base at weeks 4 and 12, and stinging at week 4 [1]. The US acne cream label lists itching, burning, stinging and tingling in about 1 to 5% of people, mild and usually settling with continued use [8]. The rosacea gel label reports burning, stinging or tingling in 29% of trial participants, usually in the first few weeks [9].

> **Safety:** If it keeps stinging, the label says to cut back or pause until it settles, and to stop and see a doctor if irritation persists [8]. Keep it away from eyes and mouth [8][9]. A rare allergy can cause facial swelling or trouble breathing, and that needs urgent care [9].

In darker skin, irritation from any product can itself leave a mark [6]. A product that burns every day is working against you.

## Does azelaic acid darken skin?

We found no source saying azelaic acid darkens normal skin. The myth may come from two places. First, irritation from any treatment can leave marks, as above [6]. Second, hydroquinone, a different ingredient, can occasionally darken skin instead of lightening it [10].

The warning on azelaic acid labels runs the other way. The US labels note isolated reports of hypopigmentation (patches losing colour), and rare reports of vitiligo (a condition where patches of skin lose their colour) and small pale spots [8][9]. The labels also say it has not been well studied in people with dark complexions, and ask them to watch for colour changes and report any to a doctor [8][9].

## Azelaic acid or hydroquinone?

Hydroquinone is an ingredient that reduces pigment production. In the UK it is prescribed, in courses of a few weeks, because it can irritate and over-lighten skin [10].

- **Effect.** In a 24-week trial on darker skin, azelaic acid with glycolic acid (an exfoliating acid) did about as well as 4% hydroquinone [2]. A pooled analysis of six trials in melasma (673 people) found a larger drop on one doctor-rated severity score with 20% azelaic acid, and no clear difference in other results or side effects [5]. The authors say larger, longer studies are needed. Those were melasma trials, not marks after pimples.
- **Speed.** A 2026 review says hydroquinone gives the fastest short-term clearing [6].
- **Long-term safety.** Unsupervised hydroquinone use beyond four to six months is linked to ochronosis, a bluish-black darkening. Long use can also cause rebound darkening. The same review describes azelaic acid as suitable for long-term use [6].

If you want a fast fix, a professional can discuss options with you. Be wary of creams that promise one.

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding

> **Safety:** Ask a doctor or pharmacist before using it.

The US rosacea gel label says azelaic acid is minimally absorbed through skin, and that maternal use is not expected to expose the fetus. It also reports that very high oral doses in animals caused harm to embryos alongside toxicity to the mother, with no malformations [9]. The acne cream label says there are no adequate studies in pregnant women and it should be used only if clearly needed [8].

For breastfeeding, the cream label says so little is absorbed that milk levels are unlikely to change, but it still advises caution [8]. By comparison, the British Association of Dermatologists says hydroquinone should be avoided in pregnancy [10].

## When to see a professional

See a doctor, pharmacist or dermatologist if:

- marks are no better after about 12 weeks
- there is spreading redness, pus, fever or severe burning
- you get facial swelling or trouble breathing (get urgent care)
- patches of skin are losing colour
- it gets into your eyes and irritation persists
- a mole or patch has changed
- skin has become thin, or bruises easily
- you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding

In the UK, a GP can refer you to a dermatologist through the NHS [10].
