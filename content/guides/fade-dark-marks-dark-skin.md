This guide takes the steps one at a time, then the ingredients to choose from, and why bleaching creams are not the answer.

{{figure:four-steps-in-order}}

## Why does dark skin mark more easily?

After a pimple, a scratch, a bite, eczema or an irritating product, skin can leave a flat tan, brown or grey mark where it was inflamed [3]. The pattern is usually called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH. "Post-inflammatory" means after the skin was inflamed. "Hyperpigmentation" means extra pigment, the substance that gives skin its colour.

PIH happens in every skin tone. It is more frequent and more marked in darker skin [1], and the marks stand out against a deeper base colour [3].

This is a pattern, not a diagnosis. A flat mark that stays after a spot has healed often fits it. A doctor or pharmacist can confirm it and tell it apart from other causes of dark patches. If the darker skin is on your knees, elbows or knuckles, see our guide to [dark knees and elbows](/guides/dark-knees-and-elbows).

Common starting points include:

- acne, eczema, contact irritation, bites and injuries [3]
- an irritating skin or hair product [2]
- tight hairstyles, chemical hair treatments and fragranced products [3]
- hormone changes, including pregnancy, and some medicines [2]

One detail worth knowing: redness is harder to see on brown and black skin, and spots may look brown or purple instead [12]. Skin can be inflamed before it looks red.

## How long does it take?

- **A few shades darker than your skin:** usually 6 to 12 months once the cause stops [2].
- **Slate blue-grey or much darker brown:** can take years [2].
- **With treatment:** often faster, and starting early helps [1][2].

Most treatment studies check results after 3 to 6 months [3]. The usual outcome is partial improvement, not full clearing. One review of 48 studies found partial improvement in 85% of people using topical retinoids, and concluded that no treatment showed robust results across the board [5].

If you are working toward a date, such as graduation or a wedding, a realistic aim is no new marks, calmer skin and older marks visibly fainter. Fully gone takes longer. The goal is an even tone in your own colour. **No routine changes your natural skin colour.**

For more on timing, see [how long dark spots take to fade](/guides/how-long-dark-spots-take-to-fade).

## Step 1: stop what irritates

If you can remove the cause, many spots clear on their own [2]. Irritation can both start marks and deepen the ones you have [1][3]. If something stings or burns, stop that product. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) suggests switching to gentler products labelled fragrance-free or for sensitive skin [2].

Every extra active product is another chance to irritate. If you use many at once, you also cannot tell which one is the problem. Fewer, simpler steps are easier to read.

Scrubbing makes it worse. The NHS and the British Association of Dermatologists both say scrubbing irritates skin [11][12]. Picking or squeezing spots also aggravates them and can scar [11][12]. More inflammation is what leaves marks [1].

Peels and lasers need care on darker skin. They can leave new marks, so choose a clinician with experience treating darker skin [3][5].

## Step 2: treat the spots that cause the marks

For marks that follow acne or eczema, AAD says the skin condition must be treated first [2]. Otherwise new marks keep arriving.

For acne, the NHS lists salicylic acid and benzoyl peroxide as over-the-counter options and suggests asking a pharmacist. Prescription retinoid creams are the next step, through a GP or dermatologist [12]. If the cause is a medicine, ask the person who prescribed it before stopping it. Do not stop it on your own [2].

For marks left by pimples, see [how to remove dark spots caused by pimples](/guides/dark-spots-from-pimples-dark-skin).

## Step 3: sunscreen every day

Sun and visible light can darken existing marks and cause new ones [2]. A review of prevention studies, mostly after laser treatment, found sunscreen was the only measure that consistently prevented new marks [4].

AAD suggests:

- SPF 30 or higher, broad-spectrum (covers UVA and UVB rays) and water-resistant [2]
- a tinted formula with iron oxide, which blocks visible light [2]
- a shade that blends with your skin tone [2]
- reapplying every two hours, or after sweating or swimming [2]
- a hat, and shade between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. [2]

## Step 4: add one evening-out ingredient

Evening-out ingredients slow how much pigment skin makes, or help skin shed marked cells faster [3]. Add one at a time and judge it over 12 weeks. Strengths below are those used in studies. Products differ by country, so check the percentage on the label.

### Azelaic acid

An acid that slows the enzyme (tyrosinase) skin uses to make pigment [3]. Studies used a 15% gel and a 20% cream, and marks improved over 12 to 16 weeks [7][8]. Mild tingling or dryness is possible [3], and one trial found more side effects with it than with its comparison treatment in the first month [7]. Ask a pharmacist or doctor about pregnancy and breastfeeding.

### Retinoids

Vitamin A relatives: adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene and retinol [2]. Gels at 0.1% (tretinoin or adapalene) partly cleared post-acne marks over 12 weeks, and nightly tretinoin 0.1% over six months gave good-to-excellent improvement in half of the African American patients studied [3]. A review concludes they are safe and beneficial in skin of colour [6].

Early irritation can briefly darken marks. Using a moisturiser alongside usually helps [3].

> **Safety:** Do not use topical retinoids in pregnancy or when planning a pregnancy. This is a precaution, as very little reaches the bloodstream [10].

The NHS lists tretinoin and adapalene as prescription retinoids for acne [12], and rules differ by country.

### Niacinamide (vitamin B3)

A gentle vitamin that reduces how much pigment moves into skin cells [3]. In studies, 5% reduced brown spots over 8 to 12 weeks, while 2% did not clearly beat plain moisturiser [3][9]. Those trials were mostly on general brown spots, not PIH, so treat it as a modest add-on. It is well tolerated [9].

### Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)

It helps reduce pigment already in the skin. Studies used stabilised forms at 10% to 20%, and one 15% serum gave mild-to-moderate improvement over 12 weeks [3]. AAD also lists it [2]. Evidence is modest, so use it as an add-on.

## Why "bleaching" creams are not the answer

Unregulated skin-lightening products can inflame skin and raise the risk of marks [3]. Some imported products contain steroids or mercury that are not on the label. Steroids can cause pimples, rashes, and thin, fragile or permanently discoloured skin [2].

Mercury-containing lightening products are hazardous and banned in many countries [13]. AAD says never to put household bleach on skin [2].

Hydroquinone is an ingredient doctors prescribe in short courses. The British Association of Dermatologists says to use it for a few weeks at a time because it can irritate and over-lighten [14], and unsupervised use beyond four to six months has been linked to a bluish-black darkening called ochronosis [3].

> **Safety:** If you have used a strong steroid cream for a long time, stopping is best done with a doctor's or pharmacist's help.

## When to see a professional

See a doctor, pharmacist or dermatologist if:

- marks are not improving after about 12 weeks of a gentle routine
- you have spreading redness, pus, fever or severe burning
- a rash, reaction or mark is near your eyes
- a mole or patch has changed [14]
- your skin has become thin or fragile, or bruises easily
- dark patches appeared without a spot or rash first, or look slate-blue or grey
- you are pregnant or breastfeeding and want to use anything beyond sunscreen and gentle care

If what you are using is not helping, a dermatologist can help [2]. In the UK, a GP can refer you [14]. Where a specialist is hard to reach, a pharmacist is a good first step [12].
