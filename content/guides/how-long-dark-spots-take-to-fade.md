The sections below cover the timeline, whether anything works overnight, what speeds fading and what slows it. For the full routine, see our guide to [fading dark marks on dark skin](/guides/fade-dark-marks-dark-skin).

## The honest timeline

How long a mark lasts depends on where the colour sits.

- **In the top layer of skin:** a spot that is a few shades darker than your natural colour usually fades within 6 to 12 months [1]. Creams can reach this kind of mark more easily than deeper ones [2].
- **Deeper in the skin:** these marks often look slate blue or grey, and fading can take years [1].
- **With treatment:** often quicker, and starting early helps [2]. Most studies still see partial improvement, not a clean slate. One review of 48 studies found partial improvement in 85% of people using topical retinoids (vitamin A creams), and it found no treatment that worked reliably [3].

Darker skin tends to form these marks more often and more strongly [2][3]. It is not something you did wrong.

If you are working toward a date, a realistic aim is no new marks and older marks visibly fainter. Fully gone is usually later.

Most of these marks follow a pimple, a bite, a scratch, eczema or an irritating product, and are often called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH. That means extra pigment (the substance that gives skin its colour) left behind after skin was inflamed. This is a pattern, not a diagnosis. Acne marks follow the same timeline, and each new pimple can leave another, so the count of new spots matters as much as the old ones [2].

## Can dark spots fade overnight?

Treatment studies measure change over months, such as one 16-week trial of azelaic acid [5], and reviews report partial improvement [3]. We found no study showing marks clearing in a day or two.

Products that promise it are worth a closer look. In the US, the FDA says there are no approved or legally marketed over-the-counter skin lightening products, and its lab testing has found high mercury levels in some [7]. FDA has also received reports of rashes, facial swelling and a permanent darkening called ochronosis from hydroquinone products [7]. The World Health Organization (WHO) calls these mercury products hazardous, notes that many countries ban them, and says they are still sold online [8].

> **Safety:** The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) warns that some imported products hide steroids or mercury, and that steroids can leave skin thin, fragile or permanently discoloured [1]. AAD also says liquid bleach is harmful on spots [1].

To check a cream you already use, see how to [check a cream for steroids, mercury or hydroquinone](/guides/check-cream-steroids-mercury-hydroquinone).

## What speeds fading

1. **Stop the cause.** If the mark followed acne or eczema, treat that first, or new marks keep arriving [1][2]. Marks often fade by themselves when you stop re-injuring the skin [1].
2. **Wear sunscreen every day.** Sun and visible light can darken marks again [1]. In a review of prevention studies, sunscreen was the only measure that consistently prevented PIH, though most of those studies followed laser treatment [4]. AAD suggests SPF 30 or higher, broad-spectrum (guards against both main types of UV rays), and a tinted formula with iron oxide, a mineral pigment that also blocks visible light, reapplied every two hours outdoors [1]. See [sunscreen for dark skin](/guides/sunscreen-for-dark-skin).
3. **Add one evening-out ingredient, and wait.** These slow how much pigment skin makes. Azelaic acid is a mild acid that slows pigment production, and a small 16-week study of a 15% gel found it helped marks left by acne [5]. Niacinamide (vitamin B3) is well tolerated, with some evidence from trials on uneven pigment in general, not only acne marks [6]. Add one at a time, give it a few months before you judge it, and check the percentage on the label.
4. **See a professional for more.** A doctor can prescribe stronger treatment than the shelf offers [1][2].

> **Safety:** If you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding, ask a pharmacist or doctor before adding any active ingredient. NHS medicines guidance says topical retinoids are not to be used in pregnancy or when planning one, as a precaution [10].

## What slows it

- **Picking or squeezing spots.** The NHS says this can cause scars [9], and more inflammation means more marks [2].
- **Scrubbing.** The NHS says scrubbing can irritate skin [9], and irritation can worsen marks [2].
- **Too many products at once.** Stinging or burning means stop that product [1][2].
- **Peels and lasers.** They can help some stubborn marks [2], but some people see marks get worse after laser [3]. Choose a clinician who treats darker skin often.

For marks that follow pimples, see [dark spots from pimples on dark skin](/guides/dark-spots-from-pimples-dark-skin).

## If your spots are not fading

First check the lists above: is something still irritating the skin, are new spots still appearing, and is sunscreen going on daily? If the routine has been gentle and steady for a few months and nothing has moved, ask a professional. The colour may also sit deeper, which takes longer.

Brown or grey flat patches on the cheeks, forehead, upper lip, nose or chin that came without a spot first can have other causes. One example is melasma, a patch pattern linked to sun and hormones that often returns after treatment stops [11]. A doctor can tell which it is.

**No routine changes your natural skin colour.** The aim is an even tone in your own colour, with fewer and fainter marks.

## When to see a professional

See a doctor, pharmacist or dermatologist if:

- marks are not improving after a few months of a gentle routine [1]
- there is spreading redness, pus, fever or severe burning
- a spot or patch is near your eyes
- a mole or patch has changed [11]
- your skin has become thin or fragile, or bruises easily [1]
- dark patches appeared without a spot or rash first
- you are pregnant or breastfeeding and want to use anything beyond sunscreen and gentle care
- you have used a strong steroid or lightening cream for a long time and want to stop, which is best done with professional help

In the UK, a GP can refer you to a dermatologist [11]. Where a specialist is hard to reach, a pharmacist is a good first step.
